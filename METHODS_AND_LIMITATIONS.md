# Methods and Limitations

The principal estimand is the change in nighttime luminosity for municipalities classified as exposed to Hurricane Mitch relative to the contemporaneous change in municipalities outside the selected exposure threshold. The event-study specifications compare each two-year bin with 1996–1997 while absorbing unit and calendar-year fixed effects. Standard errors are clustered at the unit level. The mortality specification is estimated on a state-year panel because the mortality exposure is state-level.

Wind exposure is based on NOAA Extended Best Track wind radii. A municipality is treated at a given threshold when at least 50 percent of its area falls inside the storm-total swath. The preferred displays use 50- and 64-knot thresholds. These treatment groups are small: in the preferred luminosity sample, four municipalities satisfy the 64-knot definition and eight satisfy the 50-knot definition. Controls for a higher threshold can therefore include municipalities exposed to lower wind thresholds. Regional spillovers, migration, reconstruction, and common infrastructure failures may also affect comparison municipalities.

The outcome is the natural logarithm of one plus a municipality's DMSP-OLS stable-light sum. When multiple satellites observe a municipality-year, the project averages their stable-light sums. It does not apply an explicit cross-satellite intercalibration. Year fixed effects absorb shocks common to all units in a year but cannot eliminate geographically differential sensor or saturation problems. A recorded zero is retained as zero, although the current panel does not use the cloud-free-count field to distinguish all possible low-coverage cases. Because the dependent variable is `log(1 + lights)`, `exp(beta) - 1` is not an exact percentage change in untransformed luminosity for low-light municipalities.

The preferred sample is defined only with pre-treatment information: mean stable lights during 1992–1997 must be at least 101.79, the median among observed units. The restriction limits the influence of near-zero luminosity but changes the target population toward brighter municipalities. No condition depends on post-1998 outcomes.

The design requires that, absent Mitch, treated and comparison units would have followed parallel trends after conditioning on unit and year effects. Exposure is not random. Wind intensity correlates with geography, infrastructure, poverty, settlement patterns, and state capacity. The short and noisy pre-period, combined with very few high-wind treated municipalities, limits the power and credibility of formal pretrend tests. Failure to reject a pre-period coefficient is not proof of parallel trends.

Administrative geometry comes from modern boundary files rather than a reconstructed 1998 boundary vintage. Name matching and crosswalk decisions can therefore introduce measurement error or silently exclude historical units; the matching registry should be reviewed alongside construction code. Rainfall and mortality exposures are coarse summaries and should not be interpreted as interchangeable measures of the same shock.

Finally, luminosity is a proxy for local activity, electrification, and settlement—not income itself. Post-storm changes can reflect power-system damage, rebuilding, migration, sensor properties, or economic production. The estimates are best interpreted as dynamic differences in observed nighttime luminosity under the stated exposure definitions, not as direct estimates of GDP or welfare losses.
